# Latent Consumer Insights: Recovering the Hidden Attitudinal Drivers of Young Consumers' Spending Behaviour

## Abstract

Marketing research increasingly aims to uncover *insights* — latent needs and motives that consumers themselves cannot articulate — rather than the surface preferences people report on a questionnaire. This project operationalises that idea statistically. Using the *Young People Survey* (1,010 respondents aged 15–30, 150 self-report items), I treat a 128-item psychographic and interest battery as the observable surface and ask what small set of **latent attitudinal dimensions** generates it. Exploratory factor analysis (varimax rotation; Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin = 0.81, Bartlett χ² ≈ 40,890, *p* < .001) recovers eight interpretable latent factors, including a **Status & Assertiveness** dimension that no single survey question names directly. I then (i) segment respondents into five consumer personas via k-means on the factor scores, and (ii) regress each of seven spending-habit outcomes on the latent factors to identify *which hidden attitude actually drives consumption*. The central finding is that the latent Status & Assertiveness motive is the single strongest cross-category driver of visible/premium spending — on appearance (β = +0.43), branded clothing (+0.34), shopping malls (+0.31) and gadgets (+0.27) — and simultaneously the strongest *anti-saving* force (β = −0.22 on "I save all I can"). Sociability and a distinct "retail-therapy" (Fearful & Sentimental) motive add further, separable channels, while a Highbrow Cultural Capital dimension predicts *lower* conspicuous spending.

**Scope note.** The data are a 2013 Slovak student sample — *not* a current Gen Z / Gen Alpha panel. The contribution of this project is therefore explicitly **methodological**: a reproducible, end-to-end "market-in" pipeline that turns a raw self-report survey into named latent needs, spending-driver estimates, and actionable personas. Applying the same pipeline to a fresh contemporary panel (e.g. a Japanese Gen-Z consumer survey) is precisely the empirical step I would pursue within the lab. Read the specific numbers below as a demonstration of *what the method extracts*, not as claims about today's market.

## 1. Motivation

The Insight Design Lab frames its mission as extracting *"latent needs that even the consumers themselves are not aware of yet."* This is, at its core, a **latent-variable problem**: the things consumers report (which music they like, whether they fear public speaking, how much they enjoy shopping malls) are observable *indicators*, but the marketing-relevant construct — the underlying need or motive — is unobserved and must be *inferred*. Factor analysis is the classical statistical tool for exactly this inference: it posits that a large battery of correlated survey items is generated by a small number of unobserved latent factors, and estimates them.

This project therefore takes the lab's qualitative thesis and gives it a quantitative spine. Rather than asking consumers what they want (which, by the lab's own premise, they cannot fully report), I (1) recover latent attitudinal dimensions from a broad behavioural battery, (2) express each respondent as a point in that latent space and cluster them into personas, and (3) test which latent dimensions statistically drive real spending decisions. Steps (1)–(3) together form a "product-out → market-in" analytics loop: from raw responses to named insights to consumer-facing personas.

## 2. Related Work

- **Factor analysis in consumer research** — Exploratory factor analysis has been a staple of marketing and psychometrics since Thurstone, used to reduce large attitude/lifestyle batteries (AIO — Activities, Interests, Opinions) to interpretable psychographic dimensions. This project follows that tradition, applying varimax rotation for interpretable, near-orthogonal factors.
- **Psychographic / lifestyle segmentation** — The idea of segmenting markets by values and lifestyle rather than demographics (e.g., VALS) motivates the persona step here: personas are built on *latent attitudes*, with demographics used only for post-hoc description.
- **The Young People Survey dataset** — Collected by students of the Statistics class at FSEV UK (Comenius University, Bratislava) in 2013 and released by Sabo (Kaggle). Prior public analyses focus mostly on supervised prediction (e.g., predicting empathy or loneliness). This project instead uses it for unsupervised *insight extraction* and spending-driver modelling, which better matches a marketing-research framing.

## 3. Data

**Source.** Sabo, M. (2013), *Young People Survey*, FSEV UK / Kaggle. Slovak respondents aged 15–30, sampled through a student friend-referral design in 2013.

**Size.** 1,010 respondents × 150 self-report variables: 139 integer/ordinal (mostly 1–5 Likert agreement or preference) and 11 categorical (e.g., Gender, Education, Internet usage). The survey is thematically organised into eight blocks: Music (19), Movies (12), Hobbies & Interests (32), Phobias (10), Health habits (5), Personality/Views/Opinions (57), **Spending habits (7)**, and Demographics (10).

**Variables of interest.**
- **Outcome block — Spending habits (7 items):** `Finances` ("I save all the money I can"), `Shopping centres`, `Branded clothing`, `Entertainment spending`, `Spending on looks`, `Spending on gadgets`, `Spending on healthy eating`. All 1 (strongly disagree) – 5 (strongly agree).
- **Predictor battery (128 items):** all remaining ordinal items (music, movie, hobby, phobia, health, personality/opinion), i.e. the psychographic surface from which latent factors are estimated. Raw demographics (Age, Height, Weight, Number of siblings) and the 11 categorical variables are held out of the factor model and used only for describing personas.

**Cleaning.** Missing values are sparse (608 cells, 0.4% of the matrix). Numeric items were median-imputed; categorical items mode-imputed. No respondents were dropped.

## 4. Methodology

The pipeline (`analysis.py`) has four stages.

**4.1 Descriptive spending profile.** Means, medians and the correlation structure of the seven spending items, to establish the raw picture before any latent modelling.

**4.2 Exploratory Factor Analysis (the "latent needs" step).**
- *Factorability checks.* Bartlett's test of sphericity and the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy are computed on the 128-item battery to confirm it is suitable for factoring.
- *Number of factors.* Eigenvalues of the unrotated solution are inspected via a scree plot. The Kaiser (>1) rule badly over-extracts on a 128-item battery (36 eigenvalues > 1), so the number of factors is set to **eight** — a deliberate, interpretability-driven cut that keeps the leading dimensions while remaining nameable.
- *Estimation.* A varimax-rotated factor solution (`factor_analyzer`) is fitted. Factors are labelled *post hoc* from their strongest-loading items (see `results/factor_interpretation.txt`). Each respondent's factor scores are saved as their coordinates in latent-need space.

**4.3 Persona segmentation.** Factor scores are standardised and clustered with k-means. Silhouette scores are computed for k = 2…8; because the psychographic space is continuous, silhouette is nearly flat (≈0.10–0.11 across all k), indicating **no single "natural" number of clusters**. I therefore fix **k = 5** for interpretability and actionability, treating personas as a deliberately soft partition of a continuous space rather than as hard, well-separated types. This is stated openly as a limitation (§6).

**4.4 Spending-driver models.** For each of the seven spending outcomes, an ordinary-least-squares regression is fit on the eight standardised factor scores. Because predictors are standardised and near-orthogonal (varimax), the coefficients are directly comparable *effect sizes*: they answer "holding other motives constant, how much does a one-SD increase in this latent attitude move this kind of spending?"

## 5. Results

### 5.1 The raw spending picture

Mean agreement (1–5) across the sample:

| Spending item | Mean | Median |
|---|---|---|
| Spending on healthy eating | 3.56 | 4 |
| Shopping centres (enjoy) | 3.23 | 3 |
| Entertainment spending | 3.20 | 3 |
| Spending on looks | 3.11 | 3 |
| Branded clothing | 3.05 | 3 |
| Finances (I save all I can) | 3.02 | 3 |
| Spending on gadgets | 2.87 | 3 |

Healthy eating is the one item young consumers most consistently endorse; gadget spending the least. But these surface means say little about *why* — that requires the latent model.

### 5.2 Eight latent attitudinal dimensions

The battery is highly factorable: **KMO = 0.814** ("meritorious"), Bartlett χ² ≈ 40,890 (*p* < .001). The eight retained varimax factors (25.0% cumulative variance) are, with their defining items:

| Factor | Label | Top-loading items |
|---|---|---|
| F1 | **Highbrow Cultural Capital** | Classical music, Opera, Swing/Jazz, Art exhibitions, Theatre, Reading |
| F2 | **Fearful & Sentimental** | Fear of rats/snakes/storms/dangerous dogs, Romantic films, Shopping, Celebrities |
| F3 | **Sociable & Energetic** | Energy levels, Number of friends, Happiness, (−)Loneliness, (−)Fear of public speaking |
| F4 | **Tech, Cars & Action** | PC, Action films, Cars, Sci-fi, Science & technology, Internet, War films |
| F5 | **Conscientious & Organised** | Reliability, Thinking ahead, Prioritising workload, Keeping promises, (−)Cheating |
| F6 | **Pop-Culture Entertainment** | Animated/Fantasy films, Rock, Fun with friends, Movies, Music, Comedy |
| F7 | **Life-Science / Medical** | Biology, Chemistry, Medicine, Physics, Gardening |
| F8 | **Status & Assertiveness** | Knowing the right people, Law, Politics, Getting angry, Appearance & gestures |

Crucially, several of these — particularly **Status & Assertiveness** and **Fearful & Sentimental** — are *emergent constructs*: no single survey item asks about status-seeking or retail-therapy, yet the covariance structure reveals them. This is precisely the "latent need the consumer never states" that the analysis set out to find.

### 5.3 Which latent attitude drives spending? (the core insight)

Standardised regression coefficients of each spending outcome on the eight factors (`figures/08_spending_drivers.png`). The strongest effects:

| Latent factor | Looks | Branded | Malls | Gadgets | Entertain. | Finances (save) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **Status & Assertiveness** | **+0.43** | **+0.34** | **+0.31** | +0.27 | +0.27 | **−0.22** |
| **Sociable & Energetic** | +0.26 | +0.23 | +0.18 | +0.17 | +0.28 | −0.14 |
| **Fearful & Sentimental** | +0.30 | +0.05 | **+0.48** | −0.10 | −0.12 | +0.03 |
| **Tech, Cars & Action** | −0.01 | +0.20 | +0.01 | **+0.45** | +0.13 | +0.03 |
| **Conscientious & Organised** | +0.02 | +0.04 | +0.04 | +0.04 | −0.17 | **+0.37** |
| **Highbrow Cultural Capital** | −0.14 | −0.18 | −0.18 | −0.13 | −0.03 | +0.03 |

Four findings stand out:

1. **Status is the master driver of visible consumption.** The latent Status & Assertiveness motive has the largest coefficient on *four of seven* outcomes and is the strongest single predictor of appearance spending (+0.43) and branded-clothing spending (+0.34). It is also the strongest *negative* predictor of saving (−0.22). Status-seeking young consumers spend on what others can see and save the least — a coherent, self-reinforcing profile that no direct survey question would have surfaced.

2. **"Retail therapy" is a distinct channel.** The Fearful & Sentimental factor barely predicts branded clothing or gadgets but is by far the strongest driver of *enjoying shopping malls* (+0.48) and a strong driver of spending on looks (+0.30). Mall enjoyment is emotionally, not status, driven — a meaningfully different lever for a marketer than conspicuous consumption.

3. **Category-specific motives exist.** Gadget spending is driven overwhelmingly by the Tech/Cars/Action factor (+0.45) and almost nothing else — a clean, face-valid result that supports the model's validity.

4. **Culture suppresses conspicuous spend, and prudence drives saving.** Highbrow Cultural Capital loads *negatively* on every material category; Conscientious & Organised is the near-exclusive driver of saving (+0.37) and healthy-eating spend (+0.22).

Explained variance confirms the ordering: spending on **looks (R² = 0.29)**, **shopping malls (0.26)** and **gadgets (0.21)** are the most psychographically determined, while **healthy-eating spend (R² = 0.09)** is nearly attitude-independent — a near-universal value rather than a segment signal.

### 5.4 Five consumer personas

K-means (k = 5) on the latent scores yields five balanced, interpretable personas (`figures/06_persona_factor_heatmap.png`, `figures/07_persona_spending.png`):

| # | Persona | Share | Profile | Defining spend |
|---|---|---:|---|---|
| P4 | **Trend & Retail-Therapy Shoppers** | 23.6% | 90% female; high Fearful & Sentimental, Pop-Culture, Status | **Highest** mall (3.90) & looks (3.68) spend; lowest saver |
| P1 | **Gadget-Led Individualists** | 21.3% | 88% male; high Tech/Cars/Action, low Cultural Capital | **Highest** gadget (3.31) & branded (3.29) spend |
| P2 | **Cultured Minimalists** | 19.3% | 73% female; high Cultural Capital, low Tech/Pop-Culture | **Lowest** spender across almost every category |
| P3 | **Well-Rounded Achievers** | 18.4% | Oldest, balanced gender; high on Cultural, Conscientious, Tech & Sociable | **Highest** healthy-eating spend (3.94); broad engagement |
| P0 | **Caring Science Students** | 17.4% | 78% female; dominated by Life-Science/Medical | Health-tilted, otherwise modest spenders |

The personas map cleanly onto the driver story: the two highest-spending, lowest-saving personas (P4, P1) are those loading on Status / Fearful-Sentimental and Tech respectively, while the culturally-rich P2 is the most frugal — consistent with Cultural Capital's negative spending coefficients.

## 6. Limitations

- **Sample.** 2013 Slovak student-referral sample; not representative of Gen Z / Gen Alpha in other markets or of the current year. Findings are a methodological demonstration, not a market forecast. Re-running the identical pipeline on a fresh Japanese or Gen-Z panel is the natural next step.
- **Self-report & cross-section.** Spending items are *stated attitudes*, not observed transactions; all relationships are correlational, not causal.
- **Soft clusters.** Silhouette is flat, so personas are an interpretive partition of a continuous space, not naturally separated types — they should be read as *archetypes*, not hard segments.
- **Variance explained.** Eight factors capture 25% of battery variance (normal for wide Likert batteries) and driver R² tops out at 0.29; latent attitudes are a real but partial account of spending.

## 7. Conclusion

Starting from a broad, unstructured self-report battery, the analysis recovers a compact set of latent consumer motives — most notably a **Status & Assertiveness** need that appears in no single question yet turns out to be the dominant engine of visible, premium, low-saving consumption. It separates this from an emotionally-driven "retail-therapy" motive and from category-specific drivers like tech affinity, and it packages the whole latent space into five actionable personas. This is a concrete, reproducible instance of the Insight Design Lab's central aim: converting what consumers *say* into the latent needs they *don't state*, and then into segments a marketer can act on.
